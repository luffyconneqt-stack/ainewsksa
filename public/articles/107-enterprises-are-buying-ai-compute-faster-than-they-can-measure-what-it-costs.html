107 Enterprises Are Buying AI Compute Faster Than They Can Measure What It Costs — And GCC Marketing Leaders Have a New Infrastructure Governance Gap to Close

A survey of 107 enterprises reveals AI compute spending is accelerating well ahead of cost visibility, with GPU utilisation below 50% and fewer than half of organisations tracking unit economics rigorously.
July 18, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

A new survey spanning 107 enterprises has produced a finding that should concern any GCC marketing or growth leader who has signed off on an AI infrastructure budget in the past twelve months: organisations are committing capital to AI compute at a pace that their internal measurement and governance capabilities cannot match. The compute is being bought. The visibility into what it actually costs — and whether it is being used — is not keeping up.

The Core Finding: Speed Without Sight

According to the survey, reported by VentureBeat, the majority of enterprises currently run their AI workloads on a base of hyperscalers and model-provider APIs — the familiar stack of large cloud vendors and foundational model endpoints. That is not where the next dollar is going. A majority of respondents said they intend to switch or add compute providers within the year, with a significant portion planning to act within a single quarter. The destination is specialised compute infrastructure that almost none of them currently use.

The buying criteria they cite are integration capability and total cost of ownership — not headline token price. That framing sounds disciplined. The problem is that fewer than half of the surveyed organisations rigorously track what their compute actually costs at the unit level. GPU utilisation across the sample sits at half capacity or below. Organisations are, in aggregate, paying for compute headroom they are not using, while simultaneously planning to procure more.

VentureBeat frames this as a "compute gap": heavy, fast-moving investment running ahead of the visibility needed to control it.

Why This Is a Marketing and Growth Problem, Not Just a Finance One

The instinct is to file this under infrastructure or IT. That instinct is wrong, at least for organisations where AI pipelines are embedded in customer-facing functions — personalisation engines, Arabic-language chatbots, campaign optimisation, lead scoring, dynamic pricing. In those environments, compute spend is marketing spend, just routed through a different budget line.

The implication for GCC marketing and growth leaders is direct: if fewer than half of surveyed enterprises globally can see their AI unit economics clearly, the probability that a regional marketing team has clean cost-per-inference or cost-per-campaign-decision data is low. Without that visibility, scaling an AI-driven growth function is not a strategy — it is an accumulation of unpriced operational risk.

The KSA market adds a layer of pressure here. Vision 2030 alignment has made AI investment a competitive and reputational signal for enterprises across sectors, from retail banking to e-commerce to telecoms. The incentive to show AI deployment has, in some cases, moved faster than the incentive to show AI efficiency. That gap is now closing — and organisations without cost governance frameworks in place will find the correction uncomfortable.

Provider Switching at Scale, Without a Measurement Baseline

The survey's finding on provider switching timelines deserves particular attention. Planning to move or diversify compute providers within a quarter, while lacking rigorous unit-cost tracking, is structurally problematic. Total cost of ownership is cited as a primary buying criterion — yet TCO comparisons require a baseline measurement of current costs that most surveyed organisations do not have.

The practical consequence is that provider selection decisions are likely being made on incomplete data. Integration ease becomes a proxy for economic soundness. That may produce adequate outcomes in some cases, but it is not a defensible governance posture — particularly for GCC enterprises operating under increasing scrutiny from internal finance functions and, in regulated sectors, from external oversight bodies.

What a Governance Baseline Actually Requires

While the survey does not prescribe solutions, the shape of the problem implies what a minimum governance baseline should include. Organisations need per-workload cost attribution — knowing which AI function consumed which compute, at what cost, producing what measurable output. They need GPU utilisation reporting that surfaces idle capacity before additional procurement is approved. And they need a methodology for comparing provider costs that accounts for integration overhead, not just published token or instance pricing.

For GCC marketing teams specifically, this means working with technology and finance counterparts to treat AI compute as a tracked media category — with the same expectation of cost-per-outcome accountability that applies to paid search or programmatic display. The infrastructure is scaling. The measurement discipline has not caught up. Closing that gap is now an operational priority, not a future-state ambition.
