The AI Tool Overload Problem: How Smart Agencies Are Using AI to Test AI

With hundreds of new AI tools launching every month, GCC agencies are turning to AI itself as a systematic evaluation layer — building a meta-capability that may define competitive advantage in the region's crowded marketing stack.
May 16, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

Somewhere between the 200th and 300th new AI tool of the quarter, the evaluation process breaks down. A product manager at a Riyadh-based digital agency may spend four hours testing a generative content tool, write up internal notes, and then watch the tool either pivot its pricing model or get acqui-hired within six months. The time cost is real. The signal-to-noise ratio is brutal. And the volume is only accelerating.

According to the Marketing AI Institute, most agencies cannot test even a fraction of the AI tools that reach their inboxes. That is not a workflow problem with an obvious fix. It is a structural mismatch between the pace of AI product releases — now measured in days, not quarters — and the human bandwidth agencies can realistically dedicate to vendor evaluation.

The Scale of the Problem in MENA Context

For GCC agencies specifically, the evaluation burden carries an additional layer of complexity. Many tools marketed as enterprise-ready have limited or untested support for Arabic-language inputs, right-to-left interface logic, or the dialect variation that matters enormously in markets from Cairo to Riyadh to Dubai. A tool that performs well on English-language SEO briefs may degrade significantly when fed Gulf Arabic prompts or mixed-language creative briefs. Testing for that failure mode takes time that most agencies do not have built into their retainers.

The result is a familiar pattern: agencies default to a small set of familiar platforms — typically OpenAI's GPT-4o, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini, and whatever point solutions have accumulated enough case studies to feel safe — while the broader landscape goes largely unexamined. That conservatism is defensible but costly. Tools with genuine capability advantages in specific verticals, such as Arabic-native large language models or regional social listening platforms, go unevaluated and unadopted.

AI as the Evaluation Layer

The approach gaining traction among more analytically oriented agencies is using AI systems to conduct the first pass of tool evaluation — essentially building a meta-layer where AI assesses AI. In practice, this means constructing structured prompt frameworks that interrogate new tools against a defined set of agency-specific criteria: output quality on actual client briefs, consistency across repeated inputs, handling of brand voice guidelines, response to Arabic-language inputs, and pricing logic relative to output volume.

A structured evaluation prompt, run through a reasoning-capable model such as Claude 3.5 Sonnet or GPT-4o, can process a tool's documentation, public case studies, and API specifications and return a comparative summary in minutes rather than hours. It will not replace a human judgment call on strategic fit, but it can eliminate the bottom 60 percent of the candidate pool before a human ever opens a browser tab.

Some agencies are going further, building lightweight internal benchmarking pipelines. These feed identical test briefs — a product description in Gulf Arabic, a performance ad headline set, a long-form brand article — into multiple candidate tools simultaneously, then use a scoring rubric evaluated by a separate AI model to rank outputs. The process is not perfect. AI-evaluated AI outputs carry the risk of compounding model biases. But as a triage mechanism, it is materially faster than manual review.

Building a Scalable Stack, Not a Collection of Subscriptions

The deeper implication is architectural. Agencies that treat AI tool selection as a one-time decision or an ad hoc reaction to client requests are accumulating subscriptions, not building capability. What the systematic evaluation approach enables is something closer to a living stack — a continuously updated map of which tools perform at what tasks, tested against real briefs rather than vendor demo conditions.

For GCC agencies pitching AI-enabled services to regional enterprise clients, the ability to demonstrate a rigorous, reproducible evaluation process is itself a differentiator. Saudi Vision 2030 has accelerated demand for AI-integrated marketing services across sectors from tourism to financial services, and clients in those verticals are increasingly asking agencies not just what tools they use, but how they decide. An answer rooted in structured testing methodology carries more weight than a logo wall of vendor partnerships.

The irony is pointed but useful: the most practical response to AI tool overload may be to add more AI to the process. Not as a shortcut that bypasses judgment, but as a first-pass filter that preserves human evaluation time for the decisions that actually require it. Agencies that operationalize that distinction — between decisions that need human expertise and tasks that can be systematized — are the ones most likely to build stacks that hold up under the next wave of releases. And there will be a next wave, almost certainly before the end of the month.
