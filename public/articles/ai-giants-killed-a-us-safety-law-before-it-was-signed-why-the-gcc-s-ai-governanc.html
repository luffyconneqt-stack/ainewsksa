AI Giants Killed a US Safety Law Before It Was Signed — Why the GCC's AI Governance Window Just Got Wider

When leading AI firms refused to attend the signing of a US executive order on safety testing, forcing its cancellation, they demonstrated that the global regulatory baseline for AI is not settled — and may not be anytime soon.
May 26, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

A US executive order on AI safety testing was abruptly cancelled after top AI firm CEOs declined to attend the signing ceremony, according to Ars Technica. The administration framed the shelved order as a potential innovation "blocker," but the sequence of events tells a more pointed story: the most powerful companies in the AI industry effectively vetoed a federal safety directive before it reached a pen. That is not a routine policy retreat. It is a demonstration of where the actual leverage sits in the current US AI governance ecosystem.

What Actually Happened — and What It Signals

The specific contents of the cancelled executive order have not been fully disclosed in available reporting, but its stated focus was AI safety testing — the class of requirements that would compel developers to evaluate models for risk before deployment. The objection framing — "innovation blocker" — is the standard industry counter-argument to pre-deployment evaluation mandates. What is notable here is not the argument itself, which is familiar, but the mechanism: a coordinated CEO no-show that made the signing politically untenable. Industry didn't lobby against the order after the fact. It declined to participate in the theater of its announcement, which amounts to the same outcome with considerably less diplomatic cover for the administration.

The implication is structural. The United States does not currently have a durable, enforceable federal AI governance framework, and the episode suggests that building one will require either a political coalition that can withstand industry pressure or a triggering event — a high-profile AI-related failure — that changes the public calculus. Neither condition appears imminent. For anyone building enterprise AI strategy around the assumption that Washington will produce coherent guardrails in the near term, that assumption deserves revision.

The EU Is Not Filling the Vacuum Cleanly Either

The default fallback for enterprises seeking a Western regulatory anchor has been the EU AI Act, which does establish risk-tiered requirements and deployment obligations. But implementation timelines are long, exemptions are broad, and enforcement capacity across member states is uneven. The EU framework is real in ways the current US posture is not — but "real" and "operationally settled" are different things. Enterprises in the GCC looking to the West for a compliance template to import are looking at two incomplete pictures, not one coherent one.

The Competitive Window for GCC Governance

This is where the analysis shifts for MENA operators. The GCC — and Saudi Arabia in particular, through Vision 2030-linked digital infrastructure investment — has positioned itself as an AI adoption hub, with sovereign AI ambitions that extend well beyond procurement. The absence of a dominant Western regulatory paradigm is, from one angle, a problem: global vendors will arrive with inconsistent safety postures, and procurement teams have no external framework to lean on for due diligence. From another angle, it is an opening.

Regulators and enterprise technology leaders in the GCC are not behind the curve on AI governance. They are operating in the same undefined environment as their US counterparts, but with more institutional flexibility to define context-appropriate standards. For GCC banks, telecoms, and public-sector entities building AI-dependent marketing and growth stacks, this means the internal governance frameworks they develop now — for model evaluation, data provenance, output auditing — will not be superseded by an incoming US federal standard, because that standard does not exist and its arrival is genuinely uncertain.

What This Means for AI Procurement and Deployment Decisions

For enterprise buyers in KSA and the broader GCC, the practical consequence is that vendor safety claims warrant more scrutiny, not less, precisely because the external pressure to substantiate them has loosened. When a US executive order on safety testing can be walked back by CEO non-attendance, the implicit assumption that Western AI vendors are operating under meaningful oversight requirements is harder to sustain. Procurement processes should treat safety and testing documentation as a first-order evaluation criterion, not a checkbox.

The broader strategic read is this: the global AI governance race is genuinely open. The GCC has the institutional mandate, the capital deployment velocity, and — as of this week — additional confirmation that it is not waiting on Western consensus that may not arrive. The question for regional enterprises is whether they use that window to define standards that serve their markets, or wait for an external framework that keeps not materialising.
