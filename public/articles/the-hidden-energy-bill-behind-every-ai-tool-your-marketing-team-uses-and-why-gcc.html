The Hidden Energy Bill Behind Every AI Tool Your Marketing Team Uses — And Why GCC Leaders Should Pay Attention

A landmark merger between two major US electrical utilities, driven explicitly by data-centre power demand, exposes the physical infrastructure cost that sits beneath every AI platform invoice — a constraint GCC marketing leaders can no longer ignore.
May 19, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

When two of the largest electrical utilities in the United States move toward a blockbuster merger and the stated rationale is data-centre demand, something significant has shifted in the economics of AI. The deal is not about grid modernisation for residential customers or renewable energy mandates. It is, according to reporting by Ars Technica, about power — specifically the extraordinary and accelerating volumes of it required to run the data centres that underpin modern AI infrastructure. The likely consequence for consumers, the reporting notes, is higher electricity bills.

That detail — higher bills for consumers downstream of infrastructure investment — is worth sitting with. Because the same logic runs, with few modifications, through the cost structure of every AI-powered marketing platform, cloud-based CRM, and generative AI tool that regional marketing teams are currently evaluating or already deploying.

The infrastructure layer most vendor pitches skip

AI vendors sell outputs: faster copy generation, sharper audience segmentation, predictive churn scores, automated campaign optimisation. What the pitch deck rarely surfaces is the physical stack underneath — the data centres, the cooling systems, the transmission infrastructure, and critically, the energy contracts that make inference at scale economically viable. The US utility merger is a visible crack in that abstraction. It makes legible what has mostly been buried in operational costs: AI is an energy-intensive industrial process, and the economics of that process are changing.

Data centres running large language models and real-time inference workloads consume power at a fundamentally different order of magnitude than the web hosting or database infrastructure that preceded them. When utility companies restructure at the corporate level in direct response to this demand signal, it is a reasonable indicator that the energy cost curve for AI compute is not a temporary anomaly — it is a structural feature of the technology.

Why this matters for GCC marketing and growth leaders specifically

The implication for MENA operators is not abstract. GCC markets are in the middle of a significant AI adoption cycle, with enterprises across Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and broader Gulf markets actively building or procuring AI-powered growth stacks. Many of these deployments rely on cloud infrastructure hosted in data centres outside the region — US and European facilities that are, in part, subject to the same energy cost pressures the Ars Technica piece describes. When upstream energy costs rise for US-based data-centre operators, those costs do not disappear. They migrate — into cloud compute pricing, into AI API call costs, into the per-seat SaaS rates that show up in quarterly software budgets.

For GCC organisations building locally or regionally hosted AI infrastructure — a priority that Vision 2030 industrial strategy and equivalent national programmes across the Gulf have explicitly encouraged — the energy equation is even more direct. The region's extreme climate creates cooling costs that are structurally higher than temperate markets. A data centre in Riyadh or Abu Dhabi operates under physical conditions that make energy efficiency harder to achieve and the cost of compute more sensitive to electricity pricing than equivalent facilities in northern Europe or the US Pacific Northwest.

What a utility merger signals about AI's cost trajectory

The Ars Technica reporting frames this as a policy and consumer-protection story — and rightly so. But read through a business strategy lens, it is also a pricing signal. When infrastructure investment of this scale is being mobilised to meet projected AI compute demand, the assumption that AI tools will continue to get cheaper as they get more powerful deserves more scrutiny than it typically receives in vendor conversations. Capability may continue to improve. Cost per unit of compute may fall in some segments. But the aggregate energy bill for AI at scale is likely to grow, and some portion of that growth will be passed through the supply chain to enterprise customers.

For marketing leaders in KSA and the broader GCC, the practical question is not whether to use AI — that decision is largely settled. The question is whether AI investment planning accounts for the infrastructure cost layer that sits beneath the software interface. Vendor contracts, cloud commitments, and build-versus-buy decisions that look favourable today may look different if energy-driven cost pressure is transmitted through the platforms you depend on.

A utility merger happening thousands of kilometres away is not an obvious agenda item for a regional CMO. But if that merger is being driven by the energy appetite of the same infrastructure your growth stack runs on, it belongs, at minimum, on the periphery of your strategic awareness.
