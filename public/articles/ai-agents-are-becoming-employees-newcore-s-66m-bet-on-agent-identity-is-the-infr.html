AI Agents Are Becoming Employees — NewCore's $66M Bet on Agent Identity Is the Infrastructure Signal Every GCC Enterprise Must Act On

A $66M raise for AI agent identity infrastructure signals that enterprise deployment has moved past capability debates — GCC marketing and IT teams now face a concrete governance gap they are not yet equipped to close.
June 15, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

When a startup raises $66 million to solve a problem most enterprise teams have not yet admitted they have, that is usually a signal worth taking seriously. NewCore, which according to TechCrunch is building identity and security infrastructure specifically for AI agents, is making a pointed argument: the next major challenge in enterprise security will not be managing people, it will be managing the agents working alongside them.

The Problem NewCore Is Betting On

The core proposition, as reported by TechCrunch, is that AI agents are beginning to function operationally like employees — accessing systems, executing tasks, and operating with a degree of autonomy across enterprise pipelines. What they lack is the identity infrastructure that human employees take for granted: persistent, verifiable identities, role-based access controls, audit trails, and the ability to be provisioned and deprovisioned in a governed way.

NewCore's framing — treating agents as employees rather than tools — is a meaningful conceptual shift. A tool does not need an identity. An employee does. When an agent is authorized to query a CRM, trigger a campaign workflow, access customer records, or interact with a payments API, the question of who that agent is, what it is allowed to do, and how its actions are logged becomes a live security question, not a theoretical one.

Why This Is Not Just an IT Problem

Marketing and growth teams in the GCC have been among the more aggressive early adopters of agent-based automation — deploying agents across customer onboarding flows, Arabic-language content pipelines, loyalty program triggers, and outbound campaign orchestration. The speed of that adoption has generally outpaced the governance frameworks sitting beneath it.

The implication for MENA operators is direct: the same teams celebrating efficiency gains from agent deployment are, in many cases, running those agents under permissioning models designed for human users or third-party software integrations — neither of which maps cleanly onto an agent that operates persistently, autonomously, and at scale. Access credentials shared across agents, no deprovisioning protocols when an agent workflow is retired, no audit log granular enough to reconstruct what an agent accessed during an incident — these are not hypothetical gaps. They are the predictable result of deploying new operational infrastructure on top of legacy identity management assumptions.

The Governance Gap Is Structural, Not a Configuration Issue

For GCC enterprises operating under data residency requirements — particularly those in financial services, government-adjacent sectors, and healthcare — the stakes of this gap are higher than in less regulated contexts. An agent with overly broad access permissions that touches customer PII, even incidentally, is an audit and compliance exposure. The question is not whether identity infrastructure for agents is eventually necessary. It is whether organizations will build it proactively or reactively, after an incident forces the conversation.

The $66M figure matters not because of what it tells us about NewCore specifically, but because of what it signals about where the broader market is. Institutional investors at that check size are not funding a speculative thesis — they are funding a problem they believe is already present in enterprise environments and growing. The category of agent identity management is being capitalized because the demand signal is real.

What GCC IT and Marketing Leaders Should Take From This

The immediate operational question for teams deploying agents is not which identity vendor to evaluate. It is whether the governance questions have been asked at all. How many agents currently have access to production systems? Under whose credentials? What is the deprovisioning process when a workflow changes? Is there an audit trail that satisfies both internal security and external regulatory requirements?

Most teams will find these questions uncomfortable to answer. That discomfort is the actual story here. NewCore's raise is a market signal that the capability phase of enterprise AI adoption — the period defined by "what can agents do?" — is giving way to the infrastructure phase, defined by "how do we govern what agents are doing?" For GCC enterprises that moved fast on deployment, the governance work is overdue. The fact that a well-funded startup is now dedicated to solving this problem does not mean the problem is solved. It means the problem has been confirmed.
