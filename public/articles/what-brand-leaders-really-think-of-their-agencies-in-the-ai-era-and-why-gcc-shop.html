What Brand Leaders Really Think of Their Agencies in the AI Era — And Why GCC Shops Should Be Worried

A new Marketing AI Institute report benchmarks exactly how brand leaders are evaluating agency AI capabilities — and the verdict reveals a widening gap that GCC and KSA shops cannot afford to ignore.
May 14, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

A new report from the Marketing AI Institute has done something the agency world rarely invites: it asked brand-side leaders, directly and systematically, what they think of their agencies' performance in the AI era. The findings are uncomfortable reading for shops of any size, but they carry a particular weight for agencies operating in the GCC and KSA, where client expectations around AI transformation are escalating faster than most teams are equipped to handle.

The Core Tension: Clients Are Moving, Agencies Are Lagging

The report's central finding is structural, not anecdotal. Brand leaders are not merely disappointed by individual deliverables — they are questioning whether their agencies understand AI deeply enough to lead strategic conversations at all. According to the Institute's data, a significant share of brand-side respondents say their agencies are reactive rather than proactive on AI adoption, waiting for clients to raise the topic rather than bringing informed recommendations to the table. That posture, once tolerable, is now a material reason brands consider switching partners.

The specific criticism is worth unpacking. It is not that agencies are failing to mention AI — most have learned to include the terminology. The complaint is that they cannot demonstrate applied fluency: how AI tools are changing production economics, what realistic efficiency gains look like for a given content operation, or how predictive models are reshaping media allocation decisions. Brands are arriving at briefings with sharper questions than their agencies can answer.

What Brands Actually Want (And Are Not Getting)

The report identifies several concrete capability gaps. First, brands want agencies to show evidence of internal AI adoption — not case studies borrowed from a holding company's global network, but proof that the team actually executing their account is using AI tools in its day-to-day workflow. Second, they want agencies to quantify outcomes. Phrases like "AI-powered" and "data-driven" have lost all signaling value; clients now expect agencies to name the tools, describe the workflow change, and attach a number to the result — whether that is hours saved, cost-per-asset reduced, or conversion lift measured.

Third, and perhaps most damaging for relationship longevity, brands want agencies to surface AI opportunities before being asked. The report frames this as a shift in what "strategic value" means: an agency that reacts competently is a vendor; one that anticipates and educates is a partner. The distinction is increasingly the basis on which retainers are renewed or terminated.

The GCC Dimension

For agencies operating in Saudi Arabia and the broader Gulf, these findings intersect with a specific market dynamic. Vision 2030-aligned clients — government entities, sovereign-backed ventures, and the private-sector companies orbiting them — are operating under explicit mandates to demonstrate digital and technological sophistication. An agency that cannot credibly discuss AI integration is not just behind the curve; it is potentially misaligned with the procurement criteria of the region's largest and fastest-growing accounts.

At the same time, the regional agency landscape remains heavily weighted toward creative and media execution, with AI strategy typically siloed inside consulting arms or global network hubs based in Dubai or abroad. A Riyadh-based brand team that wants applied AI counsel on, say, Arabic-language LLM fine-tuning for customer service automation, or AI-assisted Arabic content localization at scale, is unlikely to find that capability readily available at most independent or mid-tier regional shops. That gap is not hypothetical — it is the gap the Marketing AI Institute report is measuring globally, and the GCC version of it is at least as wide.

What the Data Demands of Agencies Right Now

The report stops short of prescribing a remediation path, but the implication of the data is directional. Agencies that treat AI as a marketing message rather than an operational reality are accumulating a credibility debt that periodic capability claims will not discharge. Brands are comparing notes — through industry forums, procurement networks, and the kind of informal benchmarking that accelerates in markets with concentrated advertiser pools like the GCC.

The minimum viable response is documentation: agencies need to be able to show, not tell, how AI is embedded in their production, analytics, and strategy workflows. The more demanding response is building genuine advisory capacity — people who can sit across from a CMO and have a technically literate, commercially grounded conversation about where AI creates or destroys value in their specific business context.

The Marketing AI Institute report does not name agencies or rank markets. But it provides something arguably more useful for regional operators: a clear and externally validated description of the standard brands are now applying. Whether GCC shops are meeting it is a question each leadership team will need to answer honestly — before their clients do it for them.
