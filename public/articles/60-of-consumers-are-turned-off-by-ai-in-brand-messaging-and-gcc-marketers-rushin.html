60% of Consumers Are Turned Off by 'AI' in Brand Messaging — And GCC Marketers Rushing to Publicise Their AI Credentials Have a New Trust Problem to Solve

A new WordPress VIP survey finds that six in ten US consumers are repelled by AI-labelled brand content, a structural trust fracture that carries direct implications for GCC marketers competing on AI credentials.
June 16, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

A survey published by WordPress VIP has surfaced a finding that should give pause to any marketing team currently building a campaign around its company's AI capabilities: 60% of US consumers say seeing the word "AI" in brand messaging is a turnoff. The same research notes that consumers are broadly wary of AI-generated answers even as brands increasingly treat AI-powered search as a priority referral channel.

That gap — between what brands are investing in and how consumers are receiving it — is not a messaging problem. It is a structural trust problem, and it is arriving at an awkward moment for GCC marketers who have spent the past 18 months racing to publicise AI deployments as a competitive differentiator.

The Label Has Become a Liability

The WordPress VIP data does not diagnose why consumers are repelled by AI branding, but the pattern itself is analytically significant. "AI" was, until recently, a credibility signal — it implied speed, personalisation, and technological sophistication. The survey suggests that signal has inverted, at least at the consumer-facing layer. Attaching the label to brand content now raises questions rather than answering them: Is this accurate? Was a human involved? Does this company actually understand me?

This is a familiar dynamic in technology marketing. A capability term earns trust during early adoption, then becomes noise as it saturates, and eventually becomes a liability once a critical mass of bad experiences accumulates in public memory. Chatbot failures, AI-generated misinformation incidents, and a steady stream of coverage on hallucination and bias have collectively accelerated that cycle for "AI" as a brand claim.

The implication is not that AI is losing commercial relevance — it is that the word itself is losing marketing utility, and those two things are not the same.

Why This Matters More in GCC Than in the US

The WordPress VIP survey is US-specific, and GCC consumer sentiment data on AI branding is not yet available at equivalent scale. However, there are structural reasons to expect the trust dynamic to be at least as consequential in this region, and potentially more so.

First, the credibility stakes are higher. Sectors leading AI adoption in KSA and the broader Gulf — banking, healthcare, government services, real estate — are precisely the sectors where consumers have the lowest tolerance for errors and the highest expectations of institutional accountability. A retail chatbot misfire is recoverable. An AI-labelled response from a bank or a public health platform that proves inaccurate carries reputational weight that is disproportionately large.

Second, the marketing posture across the region has been unusually forward-leaning. Vision 2030 and equivalent national programmes have created institutional pressure for organisations to demonstrate AI adoption publicly, pushing marketing teams to lead with AI credentials in contexts where a more measured approach might serve longer-term brand equity better. The WordPress VIP finding suggests that pressure may now be working against consumer engagement rather than for it.

Third, AI search as a referral channel — which the survey flags as a growing corporate priority — is still early-stage in Arabic-language digital environments. The consumer scepticism about AI-generated answers documented in the survey is relevant groundwork for any GCC brand planning to build content strategies around AI-driven discovery.

What the Data Suggests About Messaging Strategy

The actionable read from the WordPress VIP survey is not "stop using AI." It is "stop leading with the label." The underlying capabilities — faster content, more personalised responses, improved search visibility — are not the problem. The branding of those capabilities is where the trust erosion is occurring.

For GCC marketers, this points toward an approach that emphasises outcomes over infrastructure: what the customer receives, not what technology stack produced it. This is analytically uncomfortable for organisations that have made public AI investment a cornerstone of their market positioning, but the consumer data suggests the current approach is generating friction rather than confidence.

The broader implication for MENA operators is that the window for AI as a raw credibility signal may already be closing in more mature digital markets, and the region has limited lead time before that consumer posture travels. Brands that shift from AI-as-badge to AI-as-capability-in-service-of-outcomes will be better positioned when, and if, the same survey is run with GCC respondents.

The WordPress VIP survey does not tell brands to retreat from AI. It tells them to get more precise about what they are actually promising — and then deliver it without announcing the machinery behind it.
