AI Is Widening a Management Visibility Gap — And GCC Marketing Leaders Have a New Performance Framework Crisis to Solve Before AI Adoption Outpaces Every Evaluation System They Have

A new survey finding that more than half of managers struggle to evaluate AI-assisted work exposes a structural blind spot that GCC marketing leaders must address before performance management becomes the ceiling on AI-native team growth.
October 22, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

A new survey cited by TechCrunch finds that more than 50% of managers say they are finding it harder to evaluate employees' work after adopting AI tools. That single data point deserves more attention than it typically gets in the wave of productivity-optimism that surrounds enterprise AI adoption. It is not a transitional discomfort. It is a structural governance gap — and for marketing and growth leaders in the GCC who are rolling out AI tooling at pace, it represents a compounding risk that tends to surface only after the damage is done.

What the Number Actually Means

When more than half of managers report visibility loss into employee output, the performance management infrastructure of an organization is effectively degrading in real time. The traditional evaluation model — observe work, assess quality, attribute output to individual effort — breaks down when AI is generating first drafts, automating segmentation decisions, producing creative variants, and synthesizing research. The human contribution becomes harder to isolate, and the manager's calibration tools, built for a pre-AI workflow, no longer fit the inputs they are receiving.

This is not a criticism of AI adoption. It is a measurement problem, and measurement problems in organizations tend to metastasize. They distort compensation decisions, obscure genuine high performers, create political cover for low performers, and — particularly relevant to marketing functions — make it nearly impossible to attribute commercial outcomes to individual or team capability versus tool capability. For a CMO trying to justify headcount or restructure a team around AI-native roles, that ambiguity is corrosive.

Why GCC Marketing Teams Face This Pressure Acutely

The implication for MENA operators is sharper than the global average for a few reasons. First, many GCC marketing organizations are adopting AI tools rapidly, often at the direction of leadership mandates tied to national digital transformation agendas, rather than emerging organically from team-level experimentation. Top-down adoption velocity tends to outpace the institutional capacity to redesign the management systems that sit underneath the tooling.

Second, marketing functions in the Gulf frequently operate across multiple language environments, regulatory contexts, and culturally distinct consumer segments simultaneously — Arabic and English, Saudi and UAE and broader MENA. AI tools handling this complexity make individual contribution even harder to parse. When a campaign performs well in Riyadh but not in Cairo, determining whether that outcome reflects a strategist's cultural insight, a copywriter's localization judgment, or simply an algorithm's optimization choice is genuinely difficult — and currently, most performance frameworks in the region are not designed to answer that question.

Third, GCC organizations are in an active talent competition for people who can work effectively with AI at a senior level. If performance management systems cannot distinguish between an employee who uses AI skillfully and one who uses it as a crutch, organizations lose the ability to reward and retain the former. That is not a soft HR concern — it is a direct drag on the ROI of every AI investment in the workforce.

The Framework Gap No One Is Budgeting For

The survey data points to a problem that is largely absent from AI implementation budgets and project plans. Organizations are investing in licenses, training, and integration, but the parallel work of redesigning evaluation rubrics, redefining role contribution metrics, and equipping managers to assess AI-augmented output is rarely scoped as a formal workstream. It is treated as an operational detail to be resolved later.

For GCC marketing leaders, later is arriving faster than anticipated. The practical priority is to begin separating output metrics from process metrics in performance reviews — assessing not just what was produced, but the judgment, strategic input, and quality control that the human applied to AI-generated material. That requires managers to be trained differently, and it requires organizations to be honest that the old rubrics are no longer fit for purpose.

The TechCrunch survey does not offer a solution, and neither does the vendor ecosystem in any systematic way. The honest framing is that this is an organizational design problem that AI vendors have limited incentive to solve and that most HR functions have not yet been asked to prioritize. For marketing leaders in the GCC who want to scale AI-native teams without creating an evaluation vacuum, that design work needs to start now — not after the next tool rollout is complete.
