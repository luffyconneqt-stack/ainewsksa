OpenAI Acquires Ona to Power Long-Running AI Agents — And GCC Enterprise Teams Need a New Agentic Infrastructure Risk Model

OpenAI's acquisition of Ona to build secure, persistent cloud environments for AI agents consolidates the agentic stack further — raising real vendor dependency questions for GCC marketing and operations teams building multi-step AI pipelines.
June 12, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

OpenAI has announced plans to acquire Ona, with the stated goal of expanding Codex through secure, persistent cloud environments designed to support long-running AI agents across enterprise workflows. The move is operationally significant: it signals that OpenAI is no longer content to sit at the model layer and is actively building the execution infrastructure that enterprise agentic systems require to function at scale.

What the Ona Acquisition Actually Changes

Until recently, the dominant pattern in enterprise AI deployment was stateless: a model receives a prompt, returns a response, and the session ends. Agentic workflows break that pattern. A long-running agent coordinating a multi-step marketing campaign — pulling CRM data, drafting copy variants, routing approvals, logging outputs — needs a persistent environment that maintains state, handles failures gracefully, and enforces security boundaries across a potentially hours-long execution window. That infrastructure layer is exactly what Ona is being acquired to provide.

By folding this capability into Codex, OpenAI is vertically integrating upward from foundation model into runtime environment. The implication is not merely technical. Enterprises that have built agentic pipelines on top of OpenAI's models now face a vendor whose surface area — and therefore whose points of leverage and potential failure — has grown considerably larger.

Why GCC Operators Should Map This Carefully

For GCC marketing and operations teams, the timing is pointed. Adoption of multi-step AI pipelines in the region has accelerated alongside Vision 2030-linked digital transformation mandates in Saudi Arabia and equivalent initiatives across the UAE, Qatar, and Bahrain. Retailers, banks, and government-adjacent enterprises in the region have moved from experimenting with single-turn AI assistants toward deploying agents that orchestrate tasks across CRM systems, content management platforms, and customer data infrastructure.

Those teams now need to reckon with a more concentrated vendor stack than they may have anticipated when they started building. When the model provider is also the runtime environment provider, the security architecture, the uptime SLA, and the data residency question all converge on a single vendor relationship. That is a different risk profile than a modular stack where each layer can be independently audited, replaced, or failovered.

Data residency is the most acute concern for regulated sectors in the GCC. Financial institutions operating under Saudi Central Bank or UAE Central Bank frameworks, and government-linked enterprises subject to national data sovereignty requirements, will need to assess whether persistent cloud environments operated by a US-based vendor are compatible with their compliance obligations. This is not a hypothetical — it is a procurement and architecture question that agentic infrastructure now forces into the open in a way that stateless API calls largely deferred.

The Concentration Risk Is Structural, Not Incidental

It would be a mistake to frame this as a criticism specific to OpenAI. The broader pattern — foundation model providers acquiring infrastructure and tooling layers to build closed agentic ecosystems — is visible across the major labs. The Ona acquisition is simply the clearest recent signal that this consolidation is accelerating, and that enterprises which have not yet stress-tested their dependency on any single vendor's stack are overdue for that exercise.

For MENA operators specifically, the analytical posture to take is not alarm but structured reassessment. Teams building agentic pipelines should be asking: which layers of the stack are genuinely portable, and which have become load-bearing dependencies on a single provider? Where does persistent state live, and under whose security model? What does a migration path look like if pricing, policy, or availability changes at the runtime layer?

None of those questions have obvious answers yet — the Ona integration is not complete, and the technical architecture of the resulting product is not yet public. But the direction of travel is clear. OpenAI is building toward a world where enterprises run long-duration AI workloads inside OpenAI-managed infrastructure. GCC teams that are already partway down that path should treat this acquisition as a prompt to build the risk model now, before the dependency is deeper and the switching cost is higher.

The agentic infrastructure layer is no longer a neutral utility. It is a strategic asset — and the vendors who control it know it.
