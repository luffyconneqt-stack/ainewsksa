Both Sides of the AI Race Are Backed by the Same Money — What GCC Marketing Leaders Must Conclude About Vendor Lock-In

When the same VC funds back both OpenAI and Anthropic simultaneously, the winner-take-all narrative that has shaped GCC enterprise AI procurement dissolves — and vendor strategy must be rebuilt from scratch.
June 7, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

There is a single quote in a recent Wired report on frontier AI investment that should reframe how every GCC marketing technology leader thinks about their AI vendor relationships. Asked why investors are backing both OpenAI and Anthropic — companies that openly position themselves as rivals — one venture capitalist offered this: "Why wouldn't you want to be in both Pepsi and Coke? It's the same here."

That analogy deserves unpacking, because it carries a meaning the speaker may not have intended to surface quite so plainly. Pepsi and Coke are not racing to make the other obsolete. They are mature, parallel incumbents in a market that long ago settled into duopoly. When a frontier AI investor reaches for that comparison, they are signaling — whether deliberately or not — that they do not expect one lab to structurally eliminate the other. They are hedging. And sophisticated hedgers do not hedge against outcomes they consider unlikely.

The Conviction Gap That MENA Enterprises Should Notice

The investors with the deepest information access to these companies — the ones sitting on boards, reviewing internal benchmarks, and observing capability timelines firsthand — are not placing conviction bets. They are running portfolios that profit regardless of which lab leads at any given moment. This is not a sign of investor indecision. It is a structural read on the market: no single frontier model provider is considered a certainty, and the capital flowing into this space is being allocated accordingly.

For GCC and MENA enterprises, this creates a specific problem. A significant portion of AI vendor procurement over the past two years has been conducted under an implicit assumption: that the market would consolidate, that one or two labs would establish durable dominance, and that early alignment with the likely winner would yield long-term advantage. Procurement teams, technology directors, and CMOs across the region have built roadmaps around that assumption. The investment behavior reported by Wired suggests the people best positioned to validate that assumption do not share it.

What Dual-Sided Capital Structures Mean for Vendor Risk

The practical implication for MENA operators is not that all AI vendors are equivalent — they are not, and capability differences between models are real and measurable. The implication is that vendor stability and market longevity cannot be inferred from fundraising momentum alone. A lab that has raised at a high valuation and counts prominent funds among its backers may simultaneously have those same funds backing its closest competitor. The funding signal, in isolation, tells you almost nothing about which platform will still be the category leader in three years.

For marketing technology leaders specifically, this matters more than it does for, say, infrastructure procurement. Marketing AI integrations — personalisation engines, Arabic-language content generation, customer journey automation — tend to accumulate proprietary training data, workflow dependencies, and team-specific institutional knowledge around a single platform. Switching costs are high and often invisible until the moment a switch becomes necessary. Building that depth of integration on the assumption that your chosen vendor has won a race that its own investors are still hedging is a governance risk, not just a technology risk.

The Case for Architecture Over Allegiance

The rational response to a market where informed capital refuses to pick sides is to stop picking sides at the architecture level. That means designing AI-enabled marketing systems with model-agnostic middleware layers where feasible, maintaining contractual flexibility with primary vendors, and treating benchmark comparisons as ongoing operational practice rather than a one-time procurement exercise. It also means distinguishing between what is genuinely proprietary — your customer data, your Arabic-dialect training sets, your campaign performance history — and what is not, namely the model itself.

None of this argues against committing to capable tools that deliver measurable performance today. The argument is narrower: the vendor relationship should be governed as a renewable, revisable contract, not a permanent institutional alignment. The investors backing both sides of this race are, in effect, practicing exactly that discipline. The implication for GCC marketing and growth leaders is that the same discipline belongs in their procurement frameworks — before the next capability shift forces it on them anyway.
