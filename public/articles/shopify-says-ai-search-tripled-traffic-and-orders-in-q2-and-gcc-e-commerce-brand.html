Shopify Says AI Search Tripled Traffic and Orders in Q2 — And GCC E-Commerce Brands Have a New Growth Channel to Prioritise Before Competitors Lock In the Advantage

Shopify's Q2 data showing AI-driven traffic and orders tripled year-over-year is the first major commerce-layer evidence that AI search adds to e-commerce discovery rather than cannibalising it — a finding that demands immediate strategic reassessment for GCC and KSA retailers.
August 5, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

The prevailing anxiety among e-commerce operators over the past eighteen months has been that AI-powered search — ChatGPT, Perplexity, and their growing cohort of competitors — would do to product discovery what it has already done to media publishing: drain organic traffic by answering queries directly and keeping users inside the AI interface rather than sending them to merchant sites. Shopify's Q2 data, reported by TechCrunch, is the most concrete evidence to date that this narrative does not hold for commerce the way it does for content.

What Shopify's Data Actually Shows

According to TechCrunch's reporting, Shopify stated that AI-driven traffic and orders to its merchant stores tripled year over year in Q2. The company explicitly distinguished its experience from that of publishers, noting that AI is not cannibalising search traffic for its merchants the way it has for content-driven sites. That is a meaningful distinction to draw, and it is worth treating carefully: Shopify is reporting aggregate platform data, not a controlled experiment. The tripling figure captures growth from a relatively low base, and the company has commercial incentives to frame AI as an opportunity. Neither caveat negates the directional signal, but both warrant holding the headline number with appropriate analytical discipline.

The mechanism behind the divergence is plausible. When a user asks an AI assistant "what are the best running shoes for flat feet under 500 riyals," they are expressing purchase intent, not seeking editorial explanation. AI systems optimised for helpfulness have reason to surface specific products and send users to transact — the answer is incomplete without a destination. By contrast, when the same user asks "how does overpronation affect knee joints," the AI can satisfy the query entirely without a click. Publishers absorb the substitution effect. Merchants, at least at this stage, appear to receive the referral.

Why GCC Retailers Should Treat This as an Urgent Reallocation Signal

The implication for MENA operators is not simply that AI search is growing — it is that it is growing as an additive channel while traditional search budgets remain anchored to Google-centric assumptions built over a decade. GCC markets are particularly exposed to this misalignment. Smartphone and app-first commerce adoption across Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Egypt means a disproportionate share of discovery already happens outside the desktop Google paradigm. That same mobile-native user base is also among the fastest adopters of AI assistant interfaces in the region, a pattern consistent with broader digital adoption curves seen in KSA specifically.

For retailers on Shopify or equivalent platforms operating in the Gulf, the strategic question is no longer whether to have an AI search presence — it is whether product data, content architecture, and metadata are structured in ways that AI retrieval systems can parse and cite. AI assistants do not crawl sites the way Googlebot does. They surface products and brands that are well-represented in the training and retrieval data they draw on, which means product descriptions, review density, structured data markup, and brand mentions across authoritative third-party sources matter in ways that differ meaningfully from classical SEO optimisation.

What This Does Not Yet Tell Us

Shopify's data does not break down AI traffic by geography, by merchant category, or by which AI platforms are driving the referrals. It does not tell us whether the tripling reflects predominantly high-income markets with mature AI assistant adoption, or whether it is evenly distributed across Shopify's global merchant base. For GCC operators, that granularity matters: a trend driven primarily by North American consumer behaviour in Q2 2025 may arrive in the Gulf on a lag, or it may already be present in undifferentiated aggregate numbers that regional merchants cannot see in their own dashboards without deliberate attribution tracking.

The absence of that breakdown is also a competitive opportunity. If most GCC retailers are still waiting for locally-sourced evidence before acting, the brands that instrument their AI traffic attribution now, test AI-optimised product content this quarter, and begin building retrieval presence across AI-indexed sources will have a structural head start that compounds as the channel grows.

Shopify's Q2 figure is one data point from one platform. But it is the first commerce-layer data point of this kind to enter the public record, and in the context of a strategic debate that has been running almost entirely on theory and publisher-side evidence, it shifts the burden of proof. The question for GCC e-commerce operators is no longer whether AI search deserves a budget line. It is how large that line should be, and what it displaces.
