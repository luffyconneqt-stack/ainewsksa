The Legal Risks AI Is Creating for Marketing Agencies — And How to Get Ahead of Them

As AI tools embed themselves into agency workflows across MENA and the GCC, a wave of unresolved legal questions around IP ownership, data privacy, and liability is building — and most agencies are not prepared.
May 13, 2026·7 min read·Conneqt

Marketing agencies across the GCC have been among the faster adopters of generative AI tools. From Jasper and Copy.ai for content generation to Midjourney and Adobe Firefly for ad creative, to platforms like Ocoya and Persado for campaign automation, AI is no longer a side experiment — it is embedded in core delivery workflows. That operational shift is outpacing the legal frameworks agencies have in place to manage it.

The IP Ownership Problem Is Not Theoretical

When an agency uses a generative AI tool to produce a visual asset or a copy deck for a client, a foundational question goes unanswered: who owns the output? In most jurisdictions, copyright law requires human authorship. The United States Copyright Office has issued guidance rejecting copyright protection for purely AI-generated work. The UAE's Federal Law No. 38 of 2021 on copyright similarly anchors protection in human creative acts, and Saudi Arabia's Copyright Law operates on comparable principles. That means AI-generated work may sit in a legal grey zone — potentially owned by no one, potentially owned by the tool vendor under their terms of service, and almost certainly not cleanly owned by the agency or the client.

This creates practical exposure. If an agency delivers a campaign built substantially on AI-generated creative and the client later tries to trademark or exclusively license that work, the agency may have already misrepresented what it was delivering. Client contracts written before 2022 almost universally do not address this scenario. Agencies need to audit those contracts now and add explicit AI disclosure and IP assignment clauses before the next brief lands.

Training Data Liability Is the Risk Nobody Is Pricing In

Generative models are trained on data scraped from the internet, including copyrighted images, text, and code. Several ongoing lawsuits in the US and EU — including Getty Images v. Stability AI and the New York Times v. OpenAI — are testing whether outputs from these models constitute derivative works of their training data. No GCC court has ruled on this question yet, but that is a function of timing, not immunity.

An agency in Riyadh or Dubai that uses Midjourney to produce a product visual for a retail client is implicitly inheriting whatever liability attaches to that model's training provenance. Most agencies are not conducting due diligence on vendor indemnification policies. Some vendors, including Adobe with its Firefly product, have built commercially safer training datasets and offer IP indemnification. Others do not. That distinction matters and should be a procurement criterion, not an afterthought.

Data Privacy Exposure Runs in Both Directions

Agencies feeding client data — customer lists, CRM exports, performance analytics — into AI platforms face a second category of risk. Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL), which came into full effect in 2024, and the UAE's Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 both impose strict requirements on data processing, cross-border transfers, and consent. When an agency uploads client audience data to a US-based AI platform for segmentation or personalization modeling, that transfer may trigger PDPL notification and compliance obligations that neither the agency nor the client has addressed.

The liability here is bilateral. The agency may be in breach of its data processing obligations to the client, and the client may be in breach of its obligations to its own customers. Agencies operating in regulated sectors — financial services, healthcare, government — face the steepest exposure, given that sector-specific data regulations layer on top of the baseline privacy laws.

Building a Governance Framework Before Regulators Mandate One

The regulatory environment across the GCC is moving. Saudi Arabia's National Data Management Office and the UAE's TDRA are both actively developing AI governance guidance. Agencies that wait for mandated frameworks will have less flexibility to design controls that fit their workflows. Those that build internal governance now will be better positioned when compliance becomes compulsory.

A practical starting point involves four actions. First, audit every AI tool in active use and map what data flows into each platform and under what terms. Second, review client contracts and insert explicit clauses covering AI use disclosure, IP ownership allocation, and data processing consent. Third, establish a vendor evaluation checklist that includes training data provenance, indemnification policy, and data residency. Fourth, designate a named internal owner — not necessarily a lawyer, but someone accountable — for AI governance decisions.

None of this requires waiting for a legal event to force the issue. The agencies that treat legal governance as a competitive differentiator, rather than a compliance cost, are the ones that will retain institutional clients as scrutiny over AI use intensifies. The window to get ahead of this is open. It will not stay open indefinitely.
