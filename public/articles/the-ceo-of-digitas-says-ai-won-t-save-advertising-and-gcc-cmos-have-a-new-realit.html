The CEO of Digitas Says AI Won't Save Advertising — And GCC CMOs Have a New Reality Check to Build Their 2026 Strategy Around

Amy Lanzi's frank Cannes Lions assessment that AI cannot rescue a structurally troubled ad industry carries pointed implications for GCC marketing leaders building their 2026 budget cases around transformation promises that may not survive contact with reality.
July 3, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

There is a particular kind of candor that only surfaces at Cannes Lions, when senior executives are relaxed enough by the setting to say what they actually think rather than what their communications teams have approved. Amy Lanzi, CEO of Digitas North America, delivered exactly that kind of candor in a live interview at the festival this year — and what she said should sit uncomfortably with every GCC chief marketing officer currently building an AI-forward 2026 investment thesis.

The core of her argument, as reported by The Verge, is blunt: AI will not save advertising. Not because the technology is insufficient, but because the industry's structural problems are not problems that AI was ever designed to solve. She goes further, suggesting that the traditional CMO role is effectively finished — and that her own job is better understood as driving measurable business results through data and analytics than as stewarding brand narratives in any conventional sense.

The Structural Argument Worth Taking Seriously

Lanzi's position deserves attention precisely because it comes from someone whose firm is a significant buyer and deployer of the advertising infrastructure she is critiquing. This is not a technology skeptic speaking from the outside. It is an industry insider identifying a gap between what AI vendors are selling — efficiency, personalization, scale — and what the advertising industry's actual problems require.

The advertising industry's core tension is not a production bottleneck that automation can resolve. It is a trust deficit between brands and consumers, a measurement crisis that has deepened as third-party data availability has contracted, and a platform concentration problem that gives a handful of large technology companies structural leverage over media pricing. None of those problems become smaller when creative assets are generated faster or media plans are optimized by machine learning. In some respects, they become larger: more content chasing the same fragmented attention, more optimization pressure on platforms that already extract significant margin from advertisers.

Why This Recalibration Matters for MENA Operators Specifically

The implication for GCC marketing leaders is sharper than it might appear from a Cannes interview about the North American market. Across Saudi Arabia and the UAE in particular, significant marketing budget allocation decisions are being made right now for 2026, many of them structured around AI productivity arguments — the promise that generative AI tools will reduce creative production costs, that AI-driven personalization will improve return on ad spend, that automation will allow leaner teams to do more with the same budget.

Some of those efficiency gains are real and measurable. The mistake is confusing operational efficiency with strategic advantage. If every competitor in a category adopts the same AI production and optimization stack, the efficiency gains cancel out competitively while the underlying structural problems of the channel — attention scarcity, measurement opacity, platform leverage — remain fully intact.

Lanzi's reframing of her own role — from brand steward to business results operator — points toward a different kind of investment prioritization. The question for a GCC CMO is not "how much AI can we deploy in our advertising workflow" but "which business outcomes are we actually trying to move, and what is the shortest causal path between our budget and those outcomes." That is a harder question to answer, and it often produces conclusions that are uncomfortable for agencies and technology vendors whose revenue models depend on continuous spend.

The CMO Role Question Has Local Relevance

The assertion that the traditional CMO role is structurally finished is worth examining carefully in the GCC context, where marketing function structures at major organizations — in banking, retail, real estate, and government-linked entities — are still being built or rebuilt. The implication is not that marketing leadership becomes less important. It is that the skill profile shifts: away from brand intuition and creative judgment, toward comfort with data infrastructure, measurement methodology, and direct accountability for commercial outcomes.

For organizations still hiring or promoting into senior marketing roles on the basis of brand-building credentials alone, that reframing carries real structural consequences for how those roles should be scoped and evaluated.

A Useful Corrective Before Budget Season

Lanzi's Cannes assessment does not argue against AI investment in marketing functions — the efficiency gains in production and workflow are genuine and worth capturing. What it argues against is the category of thinking that treats AI as a strategic answer to problems that are fundamentally structural and commercial in nature. For GCC CMOs heading into 2026 planning cycles, the most valuable thing this interview offers is a useful corrective: AI is a tool that can make a sound strategy more efficient. It cannot substitute for having one.
