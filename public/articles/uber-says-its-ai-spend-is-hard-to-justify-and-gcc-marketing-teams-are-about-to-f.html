Uber Says Its AI Spend Is 'Hard to Justify' — And GCC Marketing Teams Are About to Face the Same Reckoning

Uber's president admits the company burned through its annual AI budget in four months without being able to draw a clear line between spending and product output — a warning sign that MENA operators cannot afford to ignore.
May 26, 2026·5 min read·Conneqt

Uber reportedly exhausted its entire annual AI budget within the first four months of 2026. The company's president and chief operating officer, Andrew Macdonald, has since gone on record to say that the link between rising AI spending and meaningful product delivery simply isn't there. Speaking to the Rapid Response podcast, Macdonald said the company is not seeing a clear connection between increased token consumption — specifically for Claude Code — and features that are actually reaching consumers. "That link is not there yet," he said. "It's very hard to draw a line between one of those stats and, 'Okay, now we're actually producing 25 percent more.'"

For an enterprise of Uber's scale and technical sophistication, this is a notable admission. The company is not a late adopter hedging on AI out of caution. It has invested heavily, moved fast, and still cannot close the loop between spend and output. That is precisely what makes Macdonald's comments significant — not as a vendor critique, but as an early signal that the measurement reckoning for enterprise AI has begun.

The 'Deploy First, Measure Never' Era Is Closing

For the past two to three years, a recognizable pattern has held across global enterprises: AI tools get adopted at the team or department level, budgets accumulate through a patchwork of individual subscriptions and platform fees, and ROI tracking is deferred on the implicit assumption that productivity gains will eventually become self-evident. Uber's situation suggests that assumption is under serious pressure.

The specific dynamic Macdonald describes — high token consumption without a corresponding increase in shipped features — points to a structural problem that goes beyond any single tool. When AI usage scales faster than the workflows, evaluation criteria, and oversight mechanisms designed to capture its value, spend detaches from output almost by design. The budget grows; the accountability layer doesn't.

Why This Matters More in GCC Markets

The implication for GCC marketing and growth leaders is direct and uncomfortable. Across the region, enterprise AI adoption has accelerated sharply, driven by a combination of genuine strategic ambition and, in some cases, pressure to be seen deploying the technology. The result, in many organizations, is a sprawl of AI tool subscriptions — content generation platforms, AI-assisted media buying, automated personalization layers, chatbot infrastructure — that were procured without the measurement frameworks needed to evaluate whether they are actually moving business metrics.

Unlike a company of Uber's size, most GCC marketing operations do not have dedicated ML infrastructure teams or the technical visibility to monitor token consumption at all. The spend is often less visible, not more. Licensing fees get absorbed into broader software budgets; productivity claims from vendors go unchallenged at renewal time because no one established a baseline at deployment. Macdonald is describing a problem Uber can at least see. For many regional operators, the equivalent problem is invisible until the budget review forces the question.

What a Justification Framework Actually Requires

The pressure to justify AI ROI is arriving faster than most teams have frameworks to handle it. For GCC marketing leaders preparing for that conversation — whether with a CFO, a board, or a regional holding company — a few structural questions are worth stress-testing now.

First, is there a documented baseline? AI tools added on top of existing workflows rarely generate measurable lift if the pre-AI output was never formally measured. Without a baseline, any improvement is anecdotal. Second, is the unit of measurement right? Token consumption, as Macdonald implicitly flags, is an input metric, not an output metric. Campaign output volume, time-to-publish, cost per approved creative, and conversion rates against a control — these are the numbers that close the accountability loop. Third, is the tool solving a constrained problem or a diffuse one? AI tools deployed against specific, narrow bottlenecks — translating creative for Gulf Arabic markets, for instance, or generating first-draft performance reports — tend to produce traceable ROI. Tools deployed as general productivity enhancers rarely do.

The Productive Outcome of Skepticism

Macdonald's candor is, in a narrow sense, a useful industry service. The dominant vendor narrative around enterprise AI has been one of compounding returns and self-evident productivity gains. A senior operator at a major technology company stating publicly that the link between spend and output "is not there yet" provides cover — and arguably an obligation — for marketing leaders elsewhere to ask the same question of their own portfolios.

The AI tools that survive the coming scrutiny will be the ones that can answer a simple question: relative to a defined baseline, what did this cost, and what did it change? Teams that build that evaluative muscle now will be better positioned than those who wait for the budget crisis to force it.
