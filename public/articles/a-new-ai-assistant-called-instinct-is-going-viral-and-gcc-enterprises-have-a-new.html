A New AI Assistant Called Instinct Is Going Viral — And GCC Enterprises Have a New Agentic Privacy Risk to Build Into Every Assistant Evaluation Before Deployment

Early testers are enthusiastic about Instinct's autonomous capabilities, but its sweeping data access and ability to act on users' behalf expose a structural risk that GCC enterprises cannot afford to treat as a secondary concern.
August 25, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

An AI assistant called Instinct is generating significant early-adopter buzz, with testers describing its autonomous capabilities in unusually enthusiastic terms. According to TechCrunch, that enthusiasm is accompanied by a quieter but more consequential conversation: the assistant's broad data access, expansive terms of service, and ability to take actions on a user's behalf are surfacing privacy and security concerns that go beyond the typical fine-print complaints attached to new consumer software.

What Makes Instinct Different — and What Makes It Risky

The core of the concern, as reported by TechCrunch, is structural rather than incidental. Instinct is not a passive query tool. It is designed to act — on behalf of users, with access to data that earlier generations of AI assistants either could not reach or were not permitted to touch. Early testers who praised what the system could do also acknowledged the uncomfortable trade-offs embedded in that capability: giving an AI assistant the authority to act is meaningfully different from giving it the authority to answer.

This distinction matters because agentic AI systems — those that can initiate actions, not merely generate responses — carry a fundamentally different risk profile than chatbots or copilots. When an assistant can read, write, send, schedule, or execute on a user's behalf, every permission granted is also a potential attack surface, a compliance exposure, or an audit gap. The enthusiasm of early testers is real, but enthusiasm at the individual user level rarely accounts for what happens when that same capability is deployed inside an organization at scale.

The GCC Enterprise Context

For GCC enterprises evaluating agentic AI assistants, the Instinct case is less about Instinct specifically and more about what it represents: the leading edge of a product category that is maturing faster than enterprise procurement frameworks are adapting to it.

The implication for MENA operators is that standard AI vendor assessments — typically built around model accuracy, language support, and integration compatibility — are not sufficient for agentic tools. A different set of questions needs to be on the table before any deployment decision is made. Where does the assistant's data access begin and end? What actions can it take without explicit per-action human confirmation? What does the terms of service actually permit the vendor to do with data the assistant touches? And critically: if the assistant makes an error while acting on a user's behalf, who bears the operational and regulatory consequence?

In GCC markets specifically, these questions carry additional weight. Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law, along with data residency expectations that vary by sector and emirate in the UAE, means that an assistant's broad data access is not only a security question — it is a potential compliance event. An agentic tool operating across email, calendar, documents, and internal systems in a regulated industry such as banking, healthcare, or government services touches data categories that carry legal obligations, not just corporate risk management preferences.

The Structural Problem With "Broad Terms"

TechCrunch specifically flags Instinct's broad terms as a component of the concern, and this deserves attention independent of any single vendor. Expansive terms of service have long been a known issue in consumer software. In agentic AI, they become a materially higher-stakes problem because the assistant is not just processing a user's query — it is potentially accessing, moving, or acting upon organizational data in ways that terms written for individual consumer use may not adequately constrain.

For GCC enterprise procurement teams, the practical takeaway is not to avoid agentic AI — the productivity case for capable autonomous assistants is real and will only strengthen. The takeaway is to treat terms-of-service review and data-access scoping as non-negotiable components of the vendor evaluation process, not as post-signature legal hygiene.

What a Rigorous Evaluation Framework Looks Like

While the source focuses on Instinct as a specific product, the same pattern almost certainly applies to every agentic assistant entering the enterprise market over the next 12 to 24 months. The market is moving toward more autonomy, not less. The vendors generating the most early-adopter enthusiasm will generally be those pushing the boundaries of what the assistant can do — which is precisely where the risk surface expands.

A defensible evaluation framework for GCC enterprises should include, at minimum: a data access audit scoped to the assistant's integration points; a human-in-the-loop assessment that identifies which actions require explicit approval versus which are permitted to execute autonomously; a terms-of-service review conducted by legal counsel familiar with local data protection law; and a clear incident response protocol for cases where the assistant acts in error. None of this is novel risk management thinking. What is new is the urgency of applying it to a product category that is arriving in enterprise environments much faster than the frameworks designed to govern it.
