Google's AI Buildout Burned 37% More Electricity in a Single Year — And GCC Sovereigns Planning Data Centres Have a New Energy Cost Model to Build

Google's 2025 environmental report confirms a 37% surge in electricity consumption driven by AI data centre expansion, handing GCC sovereign investors and enterprise planners a hard new benchmark for capital modelling.
July 6, 2026·6 min read·Conneqt

Google's 2025 environmental report, covered by Ars Technica, contains a number that should stop GCC infrastructure planners mid-slide: a 37% increase in electricity consumption in a single year, attributed directly to the company's AI data centre buildout. This is not a projection or a vendor estimate. It is a disclosed operational figure from one of the world's largest compute operators, and it represents one of the most concrete cost signals the industry has produced on the true energy intensity of AI infrastructure at scale.

What the Number Actually Means

A 37% year-on-year increase in electricity draw is not a marginal operational variance — it is a structural shift in the cost base of running AI at hyperscale. The Ars Technica report notes that Google is attempting to balance this consumption growth against clean energy efforts, but the underlying consumption trajectory is the more consequential data point for anyone building or financing AI infrastructure. Clean energy offsets are a mitigation strategy; they do not change the fundamental wattage requirement of training and serving large models.

The implication for anyone reading vendor pitches about "efficient" AI infrastructure is straightforward: if a company at Google's engineering maturity — with purpose-built chips, proprietary cooling systems, and decades of data centre optimisation — saw a 37% consumption spike, smaller and less optimised operators should model considerably worse unit economics on energy, not better.

Why This Is a GCC Capital Planning Problem Right Now

Several GCC sovereign wealth funds and national development programmes have announced or are actively developing domestic AI data centre capacity. The strategic rationale — data sovereignty, AI self-sufficiency, economic diversification — is coherent. The energy cost modelling embedded in many of those business cases may not be.

The region's energy economics have historically made large-scale compute appear artificially affordable. Subsidised or low-cost electricity has allowed regional operators to underestimate true infrastructure costs in ways that hyperscalers in competitive energy markets cannot. As GCC governments simultaneously pursue net-zero commitments and AI infrastructure ambitions, those two trajectories are now in direct numerical tension. Google's disclosure makes that tension concrete: AI infrastructure does not get cheaper to run as it scales — it gets more power-hungry.

For GCC sovereigns and the private operators co-investing alongside them, the practical implication is a need to revisit energy cost assumptions in AI infrastructure models, particularly those built before 2024. Models that assumed stable or declining energy intensity per workload, or that leaned on current subsidised tariffs without accounting for long-term energy transition costs, are likely undercooked on the liability side.

The Clean Energy Offset Problem

Google's reported attempt to balance consumption growth with clean energy investment is a version of a calculation that every large AI operator is now running. The challenge, which the Ars Technica coverage implies without fully resolving, is that renewable energy procurement timelines and grid integration constraints do not move at the same speed as GPU deployment timelines. Data centres can be filled with accelerators in months; utility-scale solar or wind capacity that actually covers the incremental load takes years to commission.

For the GCC, where solar irradiance makes renewable generation genuinely competitive and where several national energy companies have the balance sheet to move quickly, this gap may be narrower than in other markets. That is a genuine regional advantage. But it is an advantage that requires active capital allocation and grid planning coordination — not a passive benefit that arrives automatically because sunlight is abundant.

The Analyst's Read

Google's 37% figure is useful precisely because it is not a forecast — it is an outcome. It gives the market a real-world anchor for what AI infrastructure consumption looks like when a major buildout actually executes. For GCC enterprises evaluating whether to build, buy, or lease AI compute capacity, and for the sovereigns capitalising those decisions, the message is not that AI infrastructure is a bad investment. The message is that the energy cost line in the model needs to be treated as a growth variable, not a fixed assumption — and that the growth rate on that variable, if Google is any guide, is aggressive.

The operators who price that in now will be in a materially better position than those who discover it at the point of the electricity bill.
