Why South Korea Became the World's Most AI-Enthusiastic Society — And What the GCC Can Copy From Its Adoption Playbook

MIT Technology Review's on-the-ground report from Seoul reveals the cultural, regulatory, and infrastructure conditions behind Korea's mass AI normalisation — and several of those conditions are already present in Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
June 15, 2026·7 min read·Conneqt

An MIT Technology Review correspondent landing in Seoul recently walked through an unmanned AI-powered immigration checkpoint — face scan, passport scan, no human officer — before boarding a subway where AI-assisted services are woven into the commute. The anecdote is brief, but it captures something that data alone rarely does: the texture of a society that has normalised AI contact at the most bureaucratic, friction-heavy touchpoints of daily life. That normalisation did not happen by accident, and its preconditions deserve careful reading by anyone involved in Vision 2030 implementation.

What the MIT Technology Review Report Actually Shows

The Technology Review piece, published under its Algorithm newsletter, frames South Korea's AI enthusiasm as a ground-level, observable phenomenon rather than a government aspiration or a vendor projection. The evidence runs from the institutional — unmanned immigration processing at a major international gateway — to the consumer and behavioural, with everyday South Koreans integrating AI tools into routine tasks in ways the correspondent found notable enough to report on arrival, before even leaving the airport. The framing is descriptive rather than prescriptive: this is what adoption looks like when it has actually happened, not when it is being planned.

What the report does not do is offer a single causal explanation. Korean AI enthusiasm is almost certainly overdetermined — a function of high-speed digital infrastructure built out over decades, a culture of intensive technology adoption, an education system that has treated digital competency as a core skill, and a regulatory environment that moved faster than many Western counterparts to permit AI deployment in public-facing government services. The immigration checkpoint alone required a set of political and legal decisions that many governments are still debating.

The Infrastructure-First Lesson

The unmanned checkpoint is not primarily an AI story. It is a data infrastructure story. Reliable biometric matching at scale requires enrolled populations, interoperable government databases, and the political will to link them. South Korea built those foundations across multiple administrations before the AI layer became viable. The implication for GCC governments is straightforward: the question is not whether to deploy AI at border crossings or government service counters, but whether the underlying identity and data infrastructure can support it without creating new failure modes. Saudi Arabia's national digital identity programmes and the UAE's Emirates ID ecosystem suggest both countries are closer to that threshold than their regional peers — but the Korean example is a useful reminder that the AI deployment decision comes last, not first.

Consumer Normalisation Is a Marketing Variable

For growth and marketing practitioners in the GCC, the more commercially relevant insight is behavioural. The Technology Review correspondent is not describing Korean consumers who tolerate AI — they are describing consumers who prefer it, or at minimum, do not register it as noteworthy. That attitudinal baseline changes the economics of AI-powered customer experience substantially. When users trust automated systems enough to move through them without friction or complaint, conversion rates, service completion rates, and customer satisfaction scores all shift in ways that justify the infrastructure investment.

GCC consumer attitudes toward AI are not identical to Korean ones, and it would be analytically sloppy to treat them as equivalent. Cultural and generational variation within Saudi Arabia and the UAE is significant. Younger, urban, highly connected demographics in Riyadh, Jeddah, and Dubai are plausibly closer to the Korean attitudinal baseline than aggregated national surveys would suggest. Marketers targeting those segments can likely test AI-first service designs at lower reputational risk than they might assume.

What the GCC Cannot Directly Copy

Institutional enthusiasm, as the Technology Review piece documents it, is a cultural output as much as a policy input. South Korea's comfort with AI in public infrastructure reflects decades of lived experience with rapid technological change, including painful economic disruptions that created a collective orientation toward adaptation. GCC societies are navigating their own version of that dynamic — the post-oil transition narrative is explicit in Vision 2030 — but the emotional and social textures are different. Adoption playbooks that rely on public trust built over time cannot be fully compressed into a single policy cycle.

The honest read of the Korean case for GCC policymakers is therefore partial. The infrastructure sequencing is transferable. The regulatory decisiveness is transferable. The consumer behaviour, at least among specific segments, may be closer than expected. The deeper cultural substrate that makes AI feel unremarkable to a Korean arriving home from a long-haul flight — that takes longer, and no policy document accelerates it on its own.
